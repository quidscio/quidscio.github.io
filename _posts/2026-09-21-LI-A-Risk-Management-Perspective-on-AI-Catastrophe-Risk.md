# A Risk Management Perspective on AI Catastrophe Risk

*Originally published on [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/risk-management-perspective-ai-catastrophe-robert-hines-nlmie).*

Here is a back-of-the-envelope exercise: we spend money to prevent disasters we do not expect to happen. What happens when we apply consistent reasoning to AI?

Former Anthropic researcher Jacob Coxon warned that AI companies are "racing straight to self-improving superintelligence and gambling with our lives." In response, Anthropic alignment lead Evan Hubinger put his personal estimate of AI causing human extinction at more than 10% within the next decade.

That is one person's judgment, not a measured probability. But it raises a practical question: what level of spending would be proportional to the risk we believe AI presents?

## Nobody knows the probability

Some leading AI figures assign substantial probabilities to very bad outcomes:

- [Dario Amodei](https://www.axios.com/2025/09/17/anthropic-dario-amodei-p-doom-25-percent), Anthropic CEO: 25% for things going "really, really badly" (September 2025).
- [Geoffrey Hinton](https://www.wbur.org/onpoint/2025/01/10/ai-geoffrey-hinton-physics-nobel-prize), AI pioneer: 10-20% for human extinction within 30 years (January 2025).
- [Yoshua Bengio](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2023-07-15/whats-your-pdoom-ai-researchers-worry-catastrophe/102591340), deep-learning pioneer: about 20% for a catastrophic outcome (July 2023).

Other leading figures are much more skeptical:

- [Yann LeCun](https://x.com/ylecun/status/2043673634363851250), who shared the 2018 Turing Award with Hinton and Bengio, describes existential risk as "essentially zero."
- [Andrew Ng, Google Brain cofounder](https://aifund.ai/insights/insights-written-statement-of-andrew-ng-before-the-u-s-senate-ai-insight-forum/), calls the risk of one hypothetical AI-takeover scenario "vanishingly small."

## What should we do?

If you believe an AI catastrophe is even less likely than a supervolcano catastrophe over the next 100 years, then sure, doing nothing is a reasonable call. If the chances are higher, we should think about AI in the context of all similar risks. The US believes similar risks include AI, asteroid or comet impact, nuclear war, climate change, pandemics, and supervolcanos.

## Put the risks on the same footing

Congress directed DHS and FEMA to assess global catastrophic and human extinction risks with reports every 10 years. RAND Corporation's 2024 assessment, prepared to support that work, examines six threats: AI, asteroid or comet impacts, nuclear war, severe climate change, pandemics and supervolcanoes.

RAND describes the likelihood of AI catastrophe as deeply uncertain. They declined to adopt cited expert panel estimates of 2-5%. To compare these risks numerically, we turn to Oxford existential risk researcher Toby Ord's The Precipice (2020). Toby charts existential catastrophe probabilities over 100 years from 2020 and expands the definition beyond human extinction to include the permanent loss of humanity's future potential. These numbers are Toby's informal judgment. He separates pandemics into naturally occurring and engineered.

The first chart places Ord's probabilities alongside relevant US federal funding. The horizontal axis illustrates confidence in the estimates, from low to high. For example, Ord estimates a one-in-a-million chance of existential catastrophe from an asteroid or comet over 100 years. In the chart's right sidebar, the federal planetary-defense budget shown is $341 million a year, covering asteroid threats of various sizes.

## What the funding comparison tells us

Generally, you would expect funding to increase proportionally with increased risk. While it "sort-of" does, notably, AI risks do not remotely follow that pattern. NIST CAISI's $11 million budget puts federal AI testing and standards funding even lower than extremely low probability events related to volcano monitoring ($37.5 million). Asteroid defense receives $341 million, nuclear nonproliferation $2.37 billion, and pandemic-related health-preparedness programs $4.82 billion.

Defense against low-probability threats such as existential catastrophe from an asteroid or comet (1 in 1 million over 100 years) receives 30 times as much funding as AI catastrophe. Meanwhile, AI estimates range from 1 in 100 to 1 in 10 or higher. In this light, is our AI risk investment hard to explain?

## What if funding were proportional to estimated risk?

Let's take nuclear nonproliferation's $2.37 billion annual budget as a reference point. What would other budgets look like if each received the same annual dollars per unit of estimated existential risk?

Ord puts AI at 10% and nuclear war at 0.1% over the same 100-year horizon. That is a 100-to-1 ratio. Applying that ratio to the nuclear budget produces an AI funding level of $237 billion a year. Even if we adopted RAND's in-text citation of 2-5%, that range puts a $100 billion annual investment on the table, about 9,000 times CAISI's current funding.

## Just Do It!

With nonproliferation as our benchmark, a 10% chance of AI catastrophe warrants $237 billion worth of concern. Even a one-in-100,000 chance over 100 years warrants more than twice CAISI's $11 million annual funding. Our response should reflect the risk we believe exists (or might exist). Does it?
